Blindspot maps automated license plate readers and surveillance cameras, warns you before you reach one, and can route you around them.

What it does

A map of the readers

Every known ALPR near you, with the direction it faces and the area its lens actually covers — not just a pin.

A warning before you reach one

Drive normally and Blindspot tells you when a reader is coming up, whether or not you asked it for directions.

Routes that go around them

Ask for directions and you get two options: the fastest route, and the one that passes the fewest cameras — each with its exact cost in minutes, so the trade is yours to make.

A record of what you've passed

An on-device log of the readers you've actually driven past. It stays on your phone.

Where the data comes from

Camera locations come from OpenStreetMap, where they're contributed and verified by the public, and are used under the Open Database License. Blindspot ships with a copy on your device and refreshes it periodically.

Public data is imperfect. A device may have been removed, moved, or recorded in the wrong place — every camera in the app can be disputed from inside it, and each one shows when it was last verified and how many people have reported it.

Privacy

There are no accounts. Your location is used on your device to show what's near you and to warn you, and your exposure log never leaves your phone. Blindspot has no analytics and no tracking.

Planning a route or searching for a destination does send those places to Apple Maps to get directions back — that part can't happen on-device. The privacy policy spells out exactly what goes where.
